Piper Chapman is arrested by the police as a suspect in a murder investigation. When she is arrested she has already been awake for over 24 hours and is under the influence of alcohol and cocaine. Piper is not sure what to do. She really doesn't want to speak to the police but she really loves her girlfriend and doesn't want her girlfriend to be upset. Piper eventually says, "I'm sorry but I am not going to speak to you about this." 
One of the police investigators then says "OK, then. If you tell us what happened we will both make sure that you never get charged with anything. You have nothing to worry about. Just tell us what you did and we promise nothing bad will happen to you."
At that point Piper decides she will tell the police that she is guilty of the murder. She figures at this point she has nothing to worry about and just wants to go home and see her girlfriend. 
After Piper gives her statement the police take her back to her cell to hold her for her first appearance in court. Crown charges her with first degree murder. 
At Piper's trial the Crown wants to lead her statement as part of their case. In order to do so they need to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that it is voluntary. Is the trial judge likely to find that the statement was given voluntarily? Why or why not? (For full marks include the answer to: will it be found to be a voluntary statement, the full proper case citation FROM THE CASE DISCUSSED IN CLASS that relates to this topic and set out 3 different reasons to support your answer) (5 marks) 

	The trial judge is most likely to not find the statement given voluntary
	
	In R. v. Oickle 2000 SCC 38, crown needs to prove beyond reasonable doubt during Voir Dire that the statement is said voluntarily, under common laaw confession rule. The accused can alleged that the statement that was taken while violating their charter rights, the accused needs to provide balance probability of breaching charter rights or if the accused don't alleged the charter right breach. In this case there is a charter right breach under section 7, where piper is given inhumane treatment. Any evidence that she gives would be seen as involuntarily as she was undergoing psychological and physical torture, where in this case if you force someone to talk, gain or lost something by not talking, which threatening to arrest her girlfriend, the statement coming out from the accused might not be truthful. Therefore there might not be a prove of beyond reasonble doubt of the statement was taken truthfully and voluntarily. 
	
	Second reason would be referencing the same case in R. v. Oickle, where a judge determining the statement admissibility should consider if there are threats or promises, oppression, operating mind of the accused or police trickery. In this caase, there is a threat made by the police officer where they threatened to arrest her girlfriend if she is not willing to talk, where they would hold her in custody as long as they can and because of Piper not willing to talk. Oppression can be seen as depreivation of clothings, warmth and sleep in this case, where it is described as Piper getting stripped naked in a cold cell with only a metal bunk to sit on. And her request of having warmer clothes and tissue. The operating mind of Piper should also be considered, as she has been awake for over 24 hours and under the influence of alcohol and cocaine. Police trickery tactics can also be seen here as they are exploiting Viper's will of wanting to leave and not have her girlfriend be arrested, and where the police told her she would never be charged with anything and a "promise" that nothing bad will happen to Piper. The Judge should strive to understand the circumstances surrounding the confession and ask if it gives rise to a reasonable doubt as to the confession coluntariness, taking into account all the aspects of the confession rule.
	
	Third reason would be refering to R. v. Hebert 1990 CANLII 118, in this case it goes through Section 7 of the Charter which accords a detained person a pre-trial right to remain silent, and the scope of that right ectends beyond he narrow formulation of the confessions rule. The rules relating to the right to remain silent adopted by our legal system, such as the common law confessions rule and the privilege against self-incrimination, suggest that the scope of thee right in the pre-trial detention period must be based on the fundamental concept of the suspect's right to freely choose whether to speak to the authorities or remain silent. The judge should adopt and approach to pre-trial interrogation which emphasizes the right of a detained person to make a meaningful choice and which permit the rejection of statements which have been obtained unfairly in circumstances that violate that right of choice. Piper originally chose to remain silent but under police tactics, pressure and treatment she is forced to give statement, the violation of Piper is established, the evidence obtained from her would likely be rejected under s. 24.2, and be considered as invovluntary statement based on the circumstances.
